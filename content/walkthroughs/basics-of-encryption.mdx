**Step 1: Look at what you're given**

The lab hands you this cipher text:

`synt{ebg13vfabgrapelcgvba}`

It's wrapped in `synt{...}`, which looks a lot like the `flag{...}` format used  

everywhere else on the platform — just with every letter shifted. That's the  

*first clue: this isn't real encryption, it's a substitution cipher.*

**Step 2: Recognize ROT13**

The lab's instructions cover the Caesar Cipher and its most common variant,  

ROT13 — a shift of exactly 13 letters. ROT13 is symmetric, so running the  

same shift twice returns the original text. Since the alphabet has 26 letters,  

shifting by 13 is its own inverse.

Check the pattern: synt → shift each letter back 13 places →

s -> f

y -> l

n -> a

t -> g

synt becomes flag. That confirms it: this is ROT13.

**Step 3: Decode it — browser method**

The fastest way for most learners:

1\.  Search "ROT13 decoder" in your browser.

2\.  Paste in synt{ebg13vfabgrapelcgvba}.

3\.  Read off the decoded plaintext.

Step 4: Decode it — terminal method

If you'd rather do it locally, tr can remap the alphabet in one line:

bash

`echo "synt{ebg13vfabgrapelcgvba}" | tr 'A-Za-z' 'N-ZA-Mn-za-m'`

This maps A-Z and a-z onto themselves shifted by 13, which is exactly what  

ROT13 does — no external tool required.

Step 5: Submit the flag

Either method produces the same plaintext:

`flag{rot13isnotencryption}`
